There is a particular pleasure in finding a used book with someone else's marginalia.
Not the aggressive underlining of a student cramming for an exam, but the quieter kind:
a question mark beside an image, a penciled bracket around a sentence that clearly stopped someone cold.

It makes the book into a record of an encounter.

## The mark as memory

We tend to think of annotation as a tool for retention—a way of holding onto what we read.
But I've come to believe that the more important function is attention. The moment you lift a pencil
to mark something, you've already decided it matters. The marking is secondary to that decision.

This is why so much highlighting goes unused. The gesture of highlighting is easy enough to perform
without genuine engagement. But writing a word or two in the margin—even just *yes* or *why?*—
requires you to have thought something.

## What to write

The most useful marginal notes I've made are rarely summaries.
They're more often connections: a name, another book, a date, a question.
The note serves as a node in a network that only exists in your own reading life.

Start small. A simple question is enough. The habit builds from there.
